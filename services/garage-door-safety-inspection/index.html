What Is a Garage Door Safety Inspection?

A garage door safety inspection is a professional check of how safely your door and opener work. A technician examines springs, cables, tracks, rollers, hinges and brackets, tests door balance, and confirms the safety sensors and automatic reversal protect against entrapment. It flags visible defects and recommends next steps. It can't guarantee that nothing will ever go wrong.

It differs from preventive maintenance, which keeps parts running smoothly, and from repair, which fixes what has failed.

Why Garage Door Safety Inspections Matter

A garage door is one of the heaviest moving parts of a home. Its sections ride on tracks, high-tension springs and cables carry the load, and the opener adds force. Worn parts, loose mounting, a failing opener or a sensor problem can turn into entrapment or sudden-movement hazards. Finding wear early gives you time to act before something fails. Inspection doesn't guarantee accident prevention.

What Is Included in a Garage Door Safety Inspection?

The actual scope depends on your door, opener and the confirmed service. A typical inspection looks at:

  • Door panel and section condition
  • Track stability, rollers, hinges and brackets
  • Spring condition assessment
  • Cable inspection, and drums where accessible
  • Counterbalance and door balance, tested by a technician
  • Opener mounting, travel and operation
  • Photoelectric sensor testing
  • Automatic reversal testing
  • Manual release condition
  • Weather seal observations
  • Final safety findings

What is not included?

An inspection doesn't automatically include spring, cable, track or opener replacement, complete door replacement, structural changes, major mechanical repairs or replacement parts. Any extra work is assessed separately and needs your approval first.

Garage Door Spring Safety Inspection

Torsion springs sit above the door, and extension springs run along the tracks. We look for visible corrosion, gaps or separation, mounting condition, unusual sounds and balance concerns. On extension systems we also check that safety cables are present and undamaged. Springs hold substantial energy. Never wind, loosen or adjust them yourself, and don't remove a frayed safety cable. See spring replacement.

Cable and Drum Safety Checks

Lift cables are checked for fraying, corrosion, slack and uneven travel, and drums for condition and cable alignment. Uneven door movement can point to a cable problem. Don't operate a door with displaced or visibly damaged cables. See cable repair.

Track, Roller and Hinge Safety

We check track stability, roller condition, hinge wear, bracket attachment, visible deformation, binding and unusual noises, and whether the hardware suits the door. Cracked rollers and loose track hardware are common findings. Tracks and brackets are under load, so we don't give adjustment instructions for them. See track repair.

Garage Door Balance and Counterbalance Testing

Balance means the springs carry most of the door's weight. An out-of-balance door feels heavy, drifts or rises unevenly, and strains the opener. Some online guides suggest owners test balance by hand. If a spring or cable is already damaged, a disconnected door can fall, so we leave balance testing and adjustment to technicians. See balance adjustment.

Garage Door Opener Safety Inspection

We check opener mounting, motor operation, drive condition (a worn belt or chain can cause erratic movement or reversing), travel limits, force settings, wall controls, remote response and the manual release. Wall controls should be mounted out of children's reach, commonly at least five feet up per manufacturer guidance. Inspection varies by model and age. Force and limit settings are for technicians or your manual. See openers and opener repair.

Photoelectric Safety Sensor Testing

Photo-eyes send a beam across the door opening. We check position, alignment, lens cleanliness, indicator lights and wiring condition, and confirm the beam stops or reverses a closing door. Many manufacturers specify mounting at about six inches above the floor or lower, so sensors mounted high can miss small children and low objects. Even correct sensors have blind spots. Never bypass or disable them. See door not closing.

Automatic Reversal Testing

There are two kinds of reversal. Contact reversal means the door backs up when it meets an obstruction. Non-contact reversal means it stops or reverses when the sensor beam is broken. Many opener manuals describe a monthly check: lay a 2x4 flat in the door's path and close the door with the opener. It should reverse on contact. Follow your manual, since requirements differ by product. Pushing the door by hand is not a valid test.

If the door fails to reverse, stop using the opener and call a professional. Don't adjust force or limit settings yourself unless your manual directs it.

Manual Release and Power Outage Safety

The manual release lets you operate the door when the opener can't. Follow the manufacturer's instructions, which usually say to use it with the door closed. Don't disengage the opener on a visibly damaged or unbalanced door, because it can drop or fly up. Some wall controls have a vacation mode that disables remotes, which can look like an opener fault. Call a professional if the release is stuck.

When Should a Garage Door Be Inspected?

Many technicians suggest a professional inspection about once a year for typical homes, with simple owner checks monthly. Manufacturer guidance, door age, usage and weather decide the real interval.

Inspect sooner after an operating problem, after a long stretch without maintenance, or before relying on a door that hasn't been used in a while.

Safety Inspections for Different Garage Door Types

Steel, insulated, aluminum and wood sectional doors, and single or double doors, differ in weight and hardware. Manually operated and automatic doors need different checks. High-lift and low-headroom systems have different track layouts, and we inspect them where supported. Call to confirm yours.

Garage Door Safety Inspection FAQs

What is a garage door safety inspection?

A professional check of a garage door's mechanical parts, counterbalance, opener safety features and visible wear. It looks for hazards and worn components and recommends next steps. It can't promise to remove every possible risk.

What does a professional garage door safety inspection include?

Typically door sections, tracks, rollers, hinges, brackets, springs, cables, drums, door balance, opener mounting and operation, photo-eye sensors, automatic reversal, manual release and weather seals. The exact scope depends on your door and opener and is confirmed when you book.

How often should a garage door be inspected?

Guidance varies. Some door makers suggest visual checks at least twice a year, industry groups suggest monthly owner checks, and many technicians suggest a professional inspection about once a year. Your manual, door age, usage and weather all matter.

Are garage door springs checked?

Yes, visually and for performance. A technician looks for corrosion, gaps, separation and mounting condition, checks safety cables on extension springs, and watches how the spring system balances the door. Owners should never adjust springs.

Are lift cables inspected?

Yes. Technicians check for fraying, corrosion, slack, uneven travel and how cables sit on the drums. Cables carry heavy tension, so a damaged cable means stop using the door and call a professional.

Is garage door balance tested?

Yes, by a qualified technician. Balance shows how well the springs support the door's weight. A hand test by an owner is risky if a spring or cable is already damaged, so we recommend leaving it to a professional.

Are opener safety sensors tested?

Yes. We check photo-eye alignment, indicator lights, lens cleanliness, wiring condition and whether the beam stops or reverses the closing door, following the manufacturer's test method. Sensors are never bypassed.

What is an automatic reversal test?

It confirms the door reverses when it meets an obstruction or the sensor beam is broken. Owners' manuals often describe a monthly check with a board laid flat in the door's path. Pushing the door by hand is not a valid test.

Does an inspection include repairs?

No. An inspection identifies issues and recommends next steps. Repairs, parts and replacements are assessed separately and need your approval first.

Can an older garage door be inspected?

Yes. Older doors may have worn parts, discontinued hardware or opener models without current safety features, so findings may include more recommendations. Condition matters more than age alone.

What should I do if my garage door fails a safety test?

Stop using the opener and the door if it behaves unsafely. Don't bypass sensors or adjust force settings yourself. Call a professional at (833) 877-2561 so the cause can be diagnosed.

How long does an inspection typically take?

It depends on the door, opener and what is found, so we don't promise a set time. Ask when you book and we'll explain what to expect for your setup.

What affects inspection complexity?

Door size and weight, spring system, opener type, door age, hardware access, existing wear, maintenance history, weather exposure and specialized configurations. Because these vary, we don't publish fixed prices.

Is a safety inspection different from a tune-up?

Yes. An inspection focuses on identifying hazards and testing safety systems. A tune-up focuses on maintenance such as lubrication and hardware care. Packages can overlap. See garage door tune-up.

When should I stop using my garage door?

Stop if a spring is broken, a cable is frayed or loose, the track is displaced, the door hangs unevenly or drifts, brackets are damaged, or the door fails to reverse. Keep people clear and call a professional.
