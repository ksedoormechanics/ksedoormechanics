What Is Single Garage Door Installation?

Short answer: Single garage door installation means measuring and fitting a door for a one-vehicle opening, typically about 8 or 9 feet wide, with matching tracks, springs, hardware and, where used, an opener. Sizes vary, so the actual opening is measured and the work is safety tested.

A "single" door refers to the width of the opening, usually for one vehicle. It is still normally a sectional door built from several hinged panels, not a single slab. Typical uses include single-car attached garages, detached garages, homes with two separate single bays, workshops and smaller residential garages. A double door spans a two-car opening and is heavier, so it needs stronger hardware.

Who Should Choose a Single Garage Door?

A single door suits a one-vehicle garage, a narrow opening, a detached garage, or a home with two independent bays, and homeowners replacing an existing single-width door. Vehicle size, door clearance on each side, and property layout matter: a larger vehicle or a need for room to open car doors may favor a wider opening, if the framing allows it. Two separate single doors also let you open one bay at a time, which can save heating loss and wear.

Opening Measurements and Clearance

The opening and the space around it decide what can be installed. Installers check:

  • Opening width and height
  • Side room for the vertical track
  • Headroom above the opening
  • Backroom behind the opening
  • Jamb and header condition
  • Floor level
  • Ceiling obstructions such as beams or lights
  • Track clearance and opener placement

Published installation guides often quote clearances on the order of several inches of side room, roughly 10 to 12 inches of headroom and about the door height plus 18 inches of backroom, but figures differ by hardware, spring type and manufacturer. Use them only as a rough guide. Where headroom is tight, low-headroom hardware may be an option; see low-headroom conversion.

Insulation and Weather Protection

Single doors come insulated and non-insulated. Insulated sections use polystyrene or polyurethane cores, and manufacturers publish R-values for the door section. That figure is not an energy-saving guarantee: the seals, the fit and the garage itself matter.

An attached garage next to living space may benefit more from insulation than a detached one. Check the bottom seal, side and top weatherstripping, and the floor beneath the door. Ice or an uneven slab can prevent a good seal in winter. See energy-efficient doors and threshold seals.

Wind and Structural Considerations

Local building requirements, the product and the framing all affect what is suitable. Some manufacturers offer wind-load-rated configurations with added reinforcement, but requirements and ratings vary by location and model, so confirm them for your property and check with your local building department where needed. The opening needs sound jambs and header support, the door needs the right reinforcement, and the installation must follow the manufacturer's specifications. See wind-resistant doors.

Springs, Tracks and Hardware

A sectional door is balanced by torsion or extension springs, with lift cables, drums, hinges, rollers, tracks, brackets and reinforcement. Torsion springs sit on a shaft above the door; extension springs run along the horizontal tracks. Hardware has to match the door's weight, size and the manufacturer's specifications, and the door must be balanced so it can be moved by hand.

Springs and cables carry dangerous stored tension. They are installed and adjusted by trained professionals, and we do not offer DIY instructions for them.

Garage Door Opener Compatibility

A new door may work with your existing opener, or it may not. The opener must be safe and in good condition, rated for the door's weight, suited to the rail length and travel, and compatible with the door's reinforcement. Safety sensors and auto-reverse need testing after installation. Low headroom or a ceiling beam can also limit opener choice, and wall-mount options exist. See garage door openers, opener installation and wall-mount openers.

How to Tell Whether a New Door Was Installed Properly

Homeowner threads and installer guides keep raising the same issues. From the ground, check for:

  • Safety sensors mounted low, facing each other and aligned
  • Even side clearance; a small even gap is normal, rubbing is not
  • Tracks that look straight and untwisted
  • Smooth, quiet travel, with no crunching from the cables
  • A door that stays in place when stopped partway
  • The opener reverses when it meets an obstruction

Wipe sensor lenses if the door will not close. Do not adjust springs, cables or track bolts yourself; call us or your installer.

Repair, Replacement or New Installation?

A worn spring, cable or roller on a sound door often suits repair. Replacement is worth considering when panels are cracked, bowed or rusted, problems repeat, hardware is obsolete, or you want better insulation or a new look. See garage door repair and new door installation.

Single Garage Door Maintenance

Safe for you: look over tracks, rollers, hinges and seals; listen for new noise; watch door travel; wipe sensor lenses; test the opener's reversal as the manual describes. For a professional: springs, cables, drums, track alignment and balance. Follow the manufacturer's maintenance guidance, and consider professional inspections.

Single Garage Door Installation FAQs

What is a single garage door?

A single garage door is a door sized for a one-vehicle opening, commonly about 8 or 9 feet wide and 7 or 8 feet high. It is usually still a sectional door made of several hinged panels. "Single" describes the width, not a one-piece panel.

What sizes are common for single-car garage doors?

Common nominal residential sizes include 8 by 7, 9 by 7 and 9 by 8 feet, and manufacturers offer other sizes. These are examples, not universal standards. Measure the actual opening rather than relying on a nominal size.

Is an 8-foot or 9-foot-wide garage door more suitable?

It depends on the opening and how you use it. A 9-foot door gives more room to park and open car doors, while 8 feet may suit a narrow bay. The size must match the existing opening or the planned framing, so measure first.

Can a single garage door be insulated?

Yes. Many single doors are offered insulated, typically with polystyrene or polyurethane cores, with manufacturer-stated R-values that vary by model. Seals and fit also affect comfort, so compare specifications rather than expecting a guaranteed energy saving.

What materials are available?

Steel is most common, with aluminum, wood, composite and fiberglass available from some manufacturers. Not every manufacturer offers every material or design. See steel, wood and composite installation.

Can a single garage door include windows?

Often yes. Many styles offer window sections in several layouts and glass types, but windows can affect weight, insulation and hardware, and availability depends on the model. Ask about options during your assessment.

Can I reuse my existing garage door opener?

Sometimes. The opener must be in good, safe condition, rated for the new door's weight, and compatible with its reinforcement and travel. Safety sensors and auto-reverse need testing. A technician can confirm. See garage door openers.

What measurements are needed?

Opening width and height, side room, headroom, backroom, floor level, and the condition of the jambs and header, plus any ceiling obstructions. Final measurements and hardware are confirmed by the installer against the manufacturer's instructions.

How long does installation typically take?

It depends on whether the old door is removed, the door type, framing and floor condition, and opener work. A single door is often a shorter job than a double, but we do not promise a fixed time. We can estimate after assessing your opening.

What affects installation complexity?

Low headroom, ceiling obstructions, damaged framing or jambs, an uneven floor, insulated or windowed doors, opener type and the need to remove an existing door. See low-headroom options where space is tight.

How should I maintain a single garage door?

From a safe distance, check tracks, rollers, hinges and seals for wear, listen for new noises, and watch for uneven travel. Test the opener's reversal as its manual describes. Leave springs and cables to a professional. See maintenance.

What is the difference between a single and double garage door?

A double door covers a two-car opening in one wide door, so it is heavier and needs stronger hardware. Single doors suit one-vehicle bays and can be operated independently. See double door installation.

Can two separate single doors replace one double door?

Sometimes, but it is a structural change. The opening needs a center support and framing, and the finished look, jambs and openers change. A professional should assess whether your wall and header allow it.

When should I replace an existing single garage door?

Consider replacement for cracked, bowed or rusted panels, repeated failures, obsolete or incompatible hardware, poor insulation or a wish for a new look. Otherwise repair may do. See garage door repair or call for an assessment.

How much does single garage door installation cost?

It depends on door size, material, insulation, windows, hardware, opener work, removing the old door and site conditions. Retail prices often cover the door only. We do not publish fixed prices; a quote follows an assessment of your opening.

Can I install a single garage door myself?

Some people do, and kits and videos are widely available, but it is a heavy, multi-step job and the springs and cables carry dangerous stored tension. We recommend professional installation, especially for spring and counterbalance work.

How can I tell if my new door was installed properly?

Look for sensors mounted low and aligned, even side clearance with no rubbing, smooth quiet travel, a door that stays put when partly open, and no scraping or crunching from cables. A small even gap at the sides is normal. Call if you see otherwise; do not adjust springs yourself.

Why won't my garage door stay closed in winter?

Ice or debris under the bottom seal, an uneven floor or a sensor or limit setting can prevent a full close. Clear safe obstructions, check sensor lenses and, if it persists, call for an inspection. A threshold seal may help; see threshold seal installation.
